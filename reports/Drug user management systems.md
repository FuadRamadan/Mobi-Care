# Build Sierra Leone a health-first, firewalled drug-care system

Botswana does not run a special software "system" for drug users. What it runs is an **institutional model**. A single statutory **Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA)**, created under the **Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 2018**, combines enforcement with a public-education and rehabilitation wing and says it treats people with addiction "as patients, not criminals". It is coordinated through a **National Drug Control Master Strategy with a Monitoring & Evaluation Framework**. Treatment is delivered by Ministry of Health hospitals and a new 50-bed rehabilitation centre in Serowe. No African country I found, and few anywhere, runs the polished national drug-user database a visitor might imagine. The systems that work are cheap and built on standards: a common admission form, a pseudonymous ID that lets people be counted without naming them, follow-up at fixed intervals, a helpline that refers only to vetted facilities, and a strict rule that health records never reach the police. Sierra Leone is in a worse position than Botswana. Most kush now contains **nitazenes** (synthetic opioids far stronger than heroin). Treatment beds have grown quickly but only **37% of discharged patients have any follow-up data**. A December 2025 practice direction brought in **30-year minimum sentences**. There is **no data protection law**. My recommendation is that the National Drug Law Enforcement Agency (NDLEA) propose a system that is **owned and hosted by the Ministry of Health and Sanitation (MoHS)**, built on the **DHIS2 Tracker** platform the ministry already uses, designed to work offline and by SMS/USSD, and backed by a written firewall: the NDLEA sees aggregate trends, hotspots and drug-composition alerts, never named patients. The evidence behind this report is uneven. Most web pages could not be opened during research, so many findings rest on search-result snippets and should be checked before they go into a formal proposal. One small courtesy point for the meeting: Sierra Leone's NDLEA is led by an **Executive Director**, not a "chairman". "Chairman" is the title used by Nigeria's NDLEA.

## Botswana runs an agency-and-strategy model, not a software platform

The first thing to know is what Botswana actually has. I found **no public record of a Sierra Leone NDLEA visit to Botswana**, and **no named Botswana drug-user registry, case-management app or treatment database**. The only Sierra Leone–Botswana institutional contact I found was unrelated: a March 2026 medicines-contaminant lab training hosted by Botswana's medicines regulator ([BoMRA](https://www.bomra.co.bw/botswana-leads-multi-nation-fight-against-deadly-medicine-contaminants/)). Before building anything, it is worth asking the NDLEA what exactly they saw or heard about. The "system" was most likely the institutional set-up described below rather than a piece of technology.

On the title: Sierra Leone's NDLEA head is the **Executive Director**. Prince Bull-Luceni was reportedly appointed to the post on 11 August 2026, after serving as Deputy Executive Director ([NDLEA-SL](https://ndlea-sl.com/); [Politico SL](https://www.politicosl.com/articles/sierra-leones-drug-agency-paralysed-mandate-usurped)). "Chairman/CEO" is the title of **Nigeria's** NDLEA head ([NDLEA Nigeria](https://ndlea.gov.ng/managementteam)). Using the right title will matter in correspondence, and it also confirms that the brief is about Sierra Leone's agency.

Botswana's model has four layers. The **legal layer** is the 2018 Act, which INCB says provides "a legal basis for the establishment of drug rehabilitation centres and the Drug Enforcement Agency" ([INCB 2018](https://www.incb.org/incb/uploads/documents/Publications/AnnualReports/AR2018/Annual_Report_Chapters/06_Chapter_III_Africa_Annual_Report_2018_E_.pdf); [BotswanaLaws](https://botswanalaws.com/consolidated-statutes/principle-legislation/illicit-traffic-in-narcotic-drugs-and-psychotropic-substances)). A snippet of a 2025/26 government news item suggests later amendments added rehabilitation and reintegration provisions, with users referred to care centres, but I could not read the full text ([Daily News 89237](https://dailynews.gov.bw/news-detail/89237)). The **institutional layer** is the DEA itself. Its Director of Education and Outreach says the agency treats people with addiction as patients and prioritises treatment and public education ([Ngami Times](https://www.ngamitimes.co.bw/synthetic-drugs-tighten-grip/)). Its deputy head says substance use disorders need "more than law enforcement interventions" ([Mmegi](https://www.mmegi.bw/ampArticle/123238)). The **strategy layer** is the National Drug Control Master Strategy plus an M&E Framework, developed through national consultations and now being implemented with UNODC support ([Daily News 84934](https://dailynews.gov.bw/news-detail/84934); [Mmegi](https://www.mmegi.bw/ampArticle/123238)). The **clinical layer** belongs to the Ministry of Health. Historically, care came from Sbrana Psychiatric Hospital and Jubilee Hospital ([Daily News 88546](https://dailynews.gov.bw/news-detail/88546)). The country's first dedicated public rehabilitation centre is in the old Sekgoma Memorial Hospital in Serowe: **50 beds (30 adult, 20 adolescent)**, free inpatient and outpatient care, detox and aftercare. It was set for a phased opening in November 2025 ([ISSUP Botswana](https://www.issup.net/national-chapters/issup-botswana/news/2025-08/sekgoma-memorial-hospital-drug-alcohol-rehab-centre-set-open-soon)). NGOs such as BOSASNET have long filled the counselling gap that primary care leaves ([UNDP desk review](https://info.undp.org/docs/pdc/Documents/BWA/5383%20UNDP%20DESK%20REVIEW%20OF%20THE%20BOTSWANA%20NATIONAL%20ALCOHOL%20POLICY%20TEXT.pdf)).

In practice, the pathway seems to run like this. A person is identified through arrest or drug testing, through outreach, or, increasingly, because **parents ask the DEA to admit their children to rehabilitation** ([Mmegi](https://www.mmegi.bw/news/drug-abuse-rampant-parents-request-more-rehabs-dea/news)). They then go to a psychiatric or clinical service for assessment and treatment, and then to NGO counselling and aftercare. This chain is my reconstruction from scattered reports, not a published procedure. I found no documented screening tools, referral forms, court diversion orders or follow-up mechanism. Money is flowing: **P41 million for DEA drug-testing equipment** ([Mmegi](https://www.mmegi.bw/news/dea-to-purchase-drug-testing-detection-equipment/news)) and a reported **P80 million for further centres in Lobatse and Maun** ([ISSUP](https://www.issup.net/node/70139)). Even so, there is **no published outcome data**, such as how many people were treated or how many relapsed, and the Serowe centre arrived nearly a decade after it was first promised ([Sunday Standard](https://www.sundaystandard.info/national-alcohol-and-drug-rehabilitation-centre-to-be-built-in-serowe/)). On data, Botswana feeds the African Union's Pan-African Epidemiology Network on Drug Use ([JIED](https://jied.lse.ac.uk/articles/10.31389/jied.179)). It also has a new **Data Protection Act 18 of 2024** that requires risk assessments for high-risk processing of sensitive data ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/?c=BW&t=law)). That gives a drug-data system clear legal footing, which Sierra Leone currently lacks.

The lesson from Botswana is about **governance more than technology**. A single agency that owns both enforcement and rehabilitation, a costed strategy with an M&E framework, and a Ministry of Health that runs the clinical side are worth copying. Botswana does not offer a software template, because it does not appear to have one.

## Africa's working pieces are cheap, standards-based building blocks

Across the continent, the parts that work are modest, and none amounts to a national digital patient registry. The table below shows what each country does well and what Sierra Leone can borrow.

| Country | What works | What to borrow |
|---|---|---|
| **Kenya (NACADA)** | Inspects and accredits rehab facilities against national standards; free 24/7 helpline **1192** refers only to accredited facilities. In January 2026 it inspected **236 facilities**, fully accredited **135** and closed **15** ([Citizen Digital](https://citizen.digital/article/nacada-closes-15-rehabilitation-facilities-for-health-standards-violations-n375800); [NACADA](https://www.nacada.go.ke/node/290)) | A facility register plus a helpline, to bring informal "detox houses" under standards |
| **South Africa (SACENDU)** | Specialist treatment centres submit a standard admission form; results are published twice a year, e.g. **7,119 admissions in July–December 2025** ([SAMRC](https://www.samrc.ac.za/sites/default/files/attachments/2026-09/Sacendu%20Research%20Update%20Jul-Dec%202025.pdf)) | A low-cost sentinel dataset that has produced trend data for decades |
| **South Africa (COSUP, Tshwane)** | Community-based opioid substitution and needle services; reached **1,513 adults at 17 sites** in 2016–2019 and treats drug use as a medical condition ([PMC7284158](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7284158/)) | Primary-care treatment for opioid dependence, directly relevant to nitazenes |
| **Tanzania and Senegal** | Methadone clinics with daily observed dosing; Senegal's CEPIAD (2014) was West Africa's first publicly funded programme ([PMC9128414](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9128414/)); at least one Tanzanian site keeps daily attendance in an electronic database ([PMC4829437](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4829437)) | A dosing register that also tracks whether patients stay in treatment |
| **Nigeria (NDLEA)** | A 2018 national survey (**14.3 million past-year users**) ([UNODC](https://www.unodc.org/unodc/en/frontpage/2019/January/nigeria--eu-and-unodc-release-first-ever-national-drug-use-survey.html)); reports **32,402 people counselled/treated, January 2021–March 2024** ([Guardian NG](https://guardian.ng/news/32402-drug-users-counselled-treated-in-3-years-nationwide-marwa/)) | A survey method; a warning that headcounts alone hide outcomes |
| **Ghana** | Progressive 2020 law (Act 1019), but regulations and the rehabilitation fund are still pending in 2026 ([GNA](https://gna.org.gh/2026/08/nacoc-presses-government-for-rehabilitation-fund/)) | A warning: a law without regulations and money changes little |
| **Liberia** | New public-health action plan with the Ministry of Health ([AllAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202512190331.html)); only **163 people** in government-funded treatment ([New Narratives](https://newnarratives.org/featured/were-failing-a-generation-experts-say-president-boakais-drug-war-lacks-urgency-as-generation-slips-through-the-cracks/)) | A neighbour facing the same kush market and a candidate partner |

Two patterns stand out. First, **enforcement agencies tend to count activity, not outcomes**. Nigeria's NDLEA reports cumulative totals of people counselled, which says nothing about who stayed off drugs or relapsed. Second, the regional data channel already exists: the ECOWAS/UNODC **West Africa Epidemiology Network on Drug Use (WENDU)** launched its 2023 report **in Freetown in September 2024** and flagged kush as a public health emergency ([ECOWAS](https://www.ecowas.int/the-2023-west-african-epidemiology-network-on-drug-use-wendu-report-statistics-and-trends-on-illicit-drug-supply-and-drug-use-officially-released/)). A Sierra Leone system should produce WENDU-ready figures automatically rather than build a parallel reporting line.

## Rich countries count people without exposing them, and registries that expose people backfire

Outside Africa, three architectures dominate, and they differ in how much they know about each person. The **US TEDS** system counts treatment *episodes*, not people, using a 19-item admission dataset. It is simple to run but cannot follow anyone over time ([SAMHSA codebook](https://www.samhsa.gov/data/system/files/media-puf-file/teds-a-2023-ds0001-info-codebook_v1.pdf)). **Australia's** national dataset adds a **statistical linkage key (SLK-581)**, a code built from letters of a person's name, date of birth and sex. It lets records for the same person be linked **without storing names** ([AIHW manual](https://www.aihw.gov.au/getmedia/284ba49b-4e2d-403b-a294-fa6cd1318fdd/aodts-nmds-data-collection-manual-2023-24_v2.pdf)). **England's NDTMS** goes furthest. It is person-level and consent-based, and keyworkers record a short **Treatment Outcomes Profile** (days of use in the past 28 days, injecting, days in work or education) at the start of treatment, every six months and at exit ([NDTMS form](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5e6f772b86650c727b2f474b/NDTMS_adult_community_combined_review_form_CDS-P_Print.pdf)). Crucially, its consent form promises: **"We will not share information about you with the police, social services or the courts"** ([NDTMS consent form](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/62fbac198fa8f504bbbe7fc8/NDTMS-patient-information-and-consent-form.pdf)). The European standard, the **Treatment Demand Indicator**, recommends the same approach: a unique, usually pseudonymised identifier per client, so people are not counted twice across centres ([EMCDDA TDI 3.0](https://www.euda.europa.eu/system/files/publications/675/EMCDDA-TDI-Protocol-3.0_392671.pdf)).

**Portugal** shows what a health-led front door can do. Since 2001, people caught with small amounts go to regional panels of legal, health and social-work professionals rather than to court, and dependent users are steered to treatment ([Transform/UNODC](https://www.unodc.org:443/documents/ungass2016/Contributions/Civil/Transform-Drug-Policy-Foundation/Drug-decriminalisation-in-Portugal.pdf)). Early results included fewer drug deaths, less HIV and lighter prison burdens ([UNSW](https://www.unsw.edu.au/newsroom/news/2010/11/busting-drug-law-fears)). Those gains depended on funded treatment, though, and the model has strained as funding lagged: **71% of panel cases now involve cannabis**, mostly non-problematic use ([TalkingDrugs](https://www.talkingdrugs.org/an-eternal-pilot-is-portugals-decriminalisation-system-frozen-in-time/)). The design lesson is to **triage**: fast-track low-risk cases and save intensive follow-up for people with dependence. The UNODC/WHO standards point the same way. Treatment should be voluntary and evidence-based, and for people in contact with the justice system it should be offered as an alternative to conviction or punishment at every stage, with **informed consent, proportionality and safeguards**, and never forced ([UNODC-WHO Standards 2020](https://www.unodc.org/documents/drug-prevention-and-treatment/UNODC-WHO_International_Treatment_Standards_March_2020.pdf); [UNODC ATI Principles](https://www.unodc.org/documents/drug-prevention-and-treatment/UNODC_ATI_Principles_2026.pdf)). Well-run drug treatment courts cut adult reoffending from roughly **50% to 38%** ([Mitchell et al., Campbell review](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:camsys:v:8:y:2012:i:1:p:i-87)).

The warnings are stark, and they are the main reason this report recommends a firewall. In **Thailand**, registered drug users were kept under police surveillance, and fear of the registry kept people away from free care ([HRW](https://www.hrw.org/news/2009/11/30/drugs-punitive-laws-policies-and-policing-practices-and-hiv/aids)). Russia's "narkouchet" registry cost people their driving licences and jobs and needed years of abstinence to leave, and clinics reportedly leaked lists to police ([openDemocracy](https://opendemocracy.net/od-russia/dmitry-lebedev/narkouchet-i-borba-s-nim)). In the **Philippines**, community drug watch lists preceded killings. Some people who signed up for treatment ended up on those lists, and former President Duterte was **arrested and surrendered to the ICC in March 2025** ([Asia Media Centre](https://asiamediacentre.org.nz/former-philippine-president-arrested-by-icc-for-deadly-war-on-drugs); [Daily Tribune](https://tribune.net.ph/2026/02/24/prosecutors-detail-drug-war-killings)). Twelve UN agencies in 2012, and thirteen in 2020, called for compulsory drug detention centres to be closed ([UN Joint Statement](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/3950265/files/JC2310_Joint_Statement6March12FINAL_en.pdf)). A system that frightens people away from treatment fails twice: patients go untreated, and the data is biased because only the most desperate show up.

## Sierra Leone has beds and a task force but no connective data layer

Sierra Leone's crisis is unusually dangerous because kush has changed chemically. Testing found **nitazenes in more than half of samples, and later reportedly in 87%**, with synthetic cannabinoids in the rest ([GI-TOC](https://globalinitiative.net/announcements/new-report-on-kush-highlights-west-africas-growing-synthetic-drugs-challenge/); [AP](https://www.clickondetroit.com/health/2025/02/25/the-main-ingredients-in-sierra-leones-kush-are-synthetic-opioids-and-cannabinoids-report-finds/)). One detected nitazene is about **25 times more potent than fentanyl** ([GI-TOC](https://globalinitiative.net/analysis/kush-in-sierra-leone/)). UNODC says kush accounts for the largest number of people in drug treatment in the country ([WDR 2025](https://www.unodc.org/documents/data-and-analysis/WDR_2025/WDR25_B1_Key_findings.pdf)). The mix shifts constantly, and UNODC warns that **test strips may miss newer nitazene variants** ([UNODC EWA](https://www.unodc.org/LSS/Announcement/Details/e69b2ff5-5b91-4eea-8e1f-802ca7ad5080)). A static list of patients is therefore not enough. The system also has to track what is in the drugs.

The response has real assets. Since the April 2024 emergency declaration, a **National Task Force on Drug and Substance Abuse (NaTFDSA)**, modelled on the COVID-19 platform, has coordinated a five-pillar strategy. One evaluation reports beds rising from **50 to 212 across six centres** between April 2024 and October 2025, but follow-up data on employment or education exists for only **673 of 1,834 discharged patients (37%)**. That evaluation is an unreviewed preprint ([Research Square](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-7870467/v1.pdf?c=1760721538000)). The centres include the Psychiatric Teaching Hospital unit at Kissy, supported by Partners In Health ([PIH](https://www.pih.org/article/how-patients-are-healing-amid-sierra-leones-drug-crisis)), a detox centre on a military base in Bo, and a Caritas centre in Freetown ([eNCA/AFP](https://www.enca.com/opinion/addicts-fight-synthetic-kush-hell-sleone-rehab-centres)). Informal "detox houses" also chain up people who cannot get care. A study of **312 suspected kush poisonings** found a median age of 24, 94% male and **9 deaths**. People who arrived more than six hours after onset, or who had overdosed before, did worse ([JIEPH/AFENET](https://afenet-journal.org/10-37432-jieph-d-25-00329/)). That is exactly the kind of signal an early-warning dashboard should surface.

Three legal facts shape the design. First, **Part IV of the 2008 National Drugs Control Act already provides for treatment assessment panels and treatment orders**, but these were never set up ([ManoReporters](https://manoreporters.com/news/health/from-criminalization-to-recoveryrethinking-sierra-leones-2008-national-drugs-control-act/); [WADPN](https://www.wadpn.org/sierra-leone)). The legal hook for a referral-to-treatment pathway therefore exists already. Second, the Chief Justice's practice direction from 1 December 2025 sends serious drug offences to the High Court with **no bail and a reported 30-year minimum** ([TalkingDrugs](https://www.talkingdrugs.org/30-year-minimum-sierra-leone-new-drug-laws-are-some-of-the-worlds-toughest/)). The cost of treatment data leaking to enforcement is therefore severe. Third, **no data protection law has been enacted**. A 2025 bill has been validated and reportedly authorised by Cabinet, but I found no record that it has passed; that claim rests on a secondary source I could not open ([dataprotection.africa](https://dataprotection.africa/sierra-leone/)). The Cyber Security and Crime Act 2021 does criminalise unauthorised access to data, which gives a legal tool against insiders who misuse records ([Act text](https://mocti.gov.sl/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Cyber-Crime-Act-2021.pdf)). If an earlier brief mentioned a "Mental Health Act 2023", treat it with caution: every source I found says the 1902 Lunacy Act still applies and a Mental Health Bill is pending ([MoHS](https://mohs.gov.sl/moh-on-the-right-path-to-fighting-mental-health-problems-in-sierra-leone/)).

The physical environment matters just as much. Sierra Leone has about **8.94 million mobile connections (around 101% of the population) but only 20.8% internet users** ([DataReportal 2026](https://datareportal.com/reports/digital-2026-sierra-leone)). Only **11% of people say their power works most or all of the time** ([Afrobarometer](https://afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/News-release-Sierra-Leoneans-call-out-governments-performance-on-electricity-Afrobarometer-ma-bh-fmh-29apr24.pdf)), and about 90% of grid customers are in Freetown ([Trade.gov](https://trade.gov/country-commercial-guides/sierra-leone-energy-infrastructure)). On the positive side, **DHIS2 already runs the national health information system and the electronic disease surveillance system** ([ResearchGate landscape](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359851552_Digital_Health_Solutions_and_State_of_Interoperability_Landscape_Analysis_of_Sierra_Leone); [eIDSR](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/381000909_Digitalizing_disease_surveillance_experience_from_Sierra_Leone)), and **6.4 million people have a National Identification Number** ([World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/africacan/unlocking-opportunity-how-digital-id-can-transform-lives-in-sierra-leone)). The NDLEA's budget was reported at **only about US$50,000** in 2024 ([Pan African Visions](https://panafricanvisions.com/2025/07/sierra-leones-dilemma-the-deadly-kush-continues-to-ravage-its-citizens/)). In short, Sierra Leone has the pieces (centres, a task force, a legal hook and a national health platform) but nothing linking them: no shared intake form, no ID that works across centres, no follow-up after discharge, no referral route from police and courts into treatment, and no feedback from drug testing to clinics.

## The recommended design: a MoHS-hosted DHIS2 system with an NDLEA window

The core recommendation is to **build one system in two compartments**. The health compartment holds identifiable patient records. It is owned by MoHS and runs on the ministry's existing DHIS2 instance. The enforcement compartment holds NDLEA seizure and supply data. The two share only aggregate or pseudonymised information under a written NDLEA–MoHS protocol. This lets the NDLEA show leadership, since it convenes the effort and gets the intelligence it needs, without its officers ever holding patient files. That separation is what will make people willing to come forward.

### Features: what the system should do

The system needs **eight functions**, each tied to a gap identified above. (1) A **standard intake form** compatible with the European TDI and South Africa's SACENDU fields: demographics, primary substance (with "kush, contents unknown" as a code that can be reclassified once lab results arrive), route of use, previous treatment, referral source, and HIV/TB status. (2) A **pseudonymous unique ID**, generated on the device from initials, birth year, sex and district plus a secret "salt" held by MoHS, in the style of Australia's SLK-581. It lets re-admissions be matched across centres without names. (3) **Brief screening and assessment** using standard tools such as DAST-10 or WHO ASSIST, whose scores sort people into low, moderate and severe bands ([DAST-10](https://cde.nida.nih.gov/system/files/DrugAbuseScreeningTest_2014Mar24.pdf)). (4) **Case management** with treatment plans, appointment scheduling and missed-visit alerts, modelled on the existing DHIS2 HIV case-management package ([FHI360](https://www.fhi360.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/resource-dhis2-tracker-metadata-package-brief.pdf)). (5) **Follow-up at 1, 3 and 6 months** using a short outcome profile like England's, to close the 63% follow-up gap. (6) A **facility register and accreditation module**, following Kenya, so that informal detox houses can be inspected and either brought up to standard or closed. (7) A **toll-free helpline on USSD, SMS and voice**, across both Orange and Africell, for anonymous help-seeking, self-referral and appointment reminders. (8) An **early-warning module** that logs drug-sample test results, overdose clusters and deaths, records whether a substance is "suspected" or "lab-confirmed", and fires alerts to clinics and emergency departments, for example to stock naloxone and use repeat doses for nitazene overdoses.

### Workflow: how a person moves through it

A person can enter through **five doors**: self-referral via the helpline, a family request (as in Botswana), an outreach or peer worker, an emergency department after an overdose, or a police or court referral under the Part IV treatment-order provisions. At every door the same steps follow. A trained worker obtains **tiered consent** in Krio, covering treatment, follow-up contact and anonymous use of data for statistics, and declining registration never blocks care. The worker runs a brief screen and assigns the pseudonymous ID. A clinician then assesses severity and places the person in outpatient care, detox or inpatient rehabilitation, with naloxone provided and, later, opioid substitution if methadone or buprenorphine is introduced. At discharge the system schedules follow-ups by SMS, phone or peer visit, and links the person to reintegration support such as education, work or family mediation. For justice referrals, the system should follow the UNODC alternatives-to-punishment principles: assessment decides eligibility, the person can decline and go through the ordinary legal process, treatment conditions are no harsher than the sanction they replace, and there is no compulsory detention. Courts receive only a yes/no attendance confirmation, never clinical notes.

### Architecture and platform

The table below compares the platform options. These are my judgements based on the research.

| Option | Strengths | Weaknesses | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DHIS2 Tracker + Android Capture** | Already the MoHS national standard; works offline; SMS fallback; granular sharing, audit logs, "break-the-glass" access with a logged reason, forced on-device encryption ([DHIS2 Security](https://dhis2.org/security)); local technical capacity | Not a full clinical records system; existing HIV/TB trackers are "not optimised" for clinical care ([DHIS2 docs](https://docs.dhis2.org/en/implement/health/hiv/hiv-case-surveillance/design/hiv-case-surveillance-tracker.html)), so a care-oriented design must be built | **Recommended core** |
| CommCare | Strong offline outreach apps; used in Sierra Leone before ([World Vision](https://www.wvi.org/publications/health/2021-digital-health-factsheet-aim-health-plus-sierra-leone)) | Licence fees; usually cloud-hosted abroad | Fallback for outreach only |
| OpenMRS | Better for inpatient clinical notes | No Sierra Leone deployment found | Later, for large inpatient units |
| KoboToolbox / ODK | Good for surveys | Weak for long-term follow-up | Use for prevalence surveys |
| Custom build | Full control | Highest risk to cost, security and sustainability | Only for thin add-ons (USSD gateway, ID service) |

Mobi-Care's natural role, if it bids for work, is in these thin layers and in implementation support: the USSD/IVR helpline gateway, the pseudonymisation service, training, and the follow-up and reminder workflow. Building a rival platform to DHIS2 would be the wrong move. One caveat matters: **I found no published example anywhere of DHIS2 being used specifically for substance-use case management**. Sierra Leone would be breaking new ground, which supports piloting before scaling.

### Data protection while there is no law

Because no data protection act exists, the system must create its own rules and design them to meet the likely future law. The safeguards are as follows. A **ministerial directive or statutory instrument plus an NDLEA–MoHS data-sharing agreement** that states treatment records cannot be used for investigations, with any court-order route kept narrow and audited. **Role-based access**: peer workers see only their own caseload, clinicians see their own facility, district staff see aggregates, national analysts see de-identified lists, NDLEA analysts see aggregates only, and system administrators cannot browse records. **Small-number suppression** on dashboards, so that counts under five are hidden and maps stop at chiefdom or ward level. Names, phone numbers and NINs are kept in an **encrypted identity store**, optional and visible only to the treating clinic, and the NIN is never the visible ID. **Encryption** in transit and at rest, device PINs and remote wipe. **Monthly audit-log reviews** by an independent MoHS data protection officer, with insider misuse prosecuted under the Cyber Security and Crime Act. **Retention limits**, so records are not permanent labels. **No community-generated lists of suspected users**, ever. A **data governance committee** that includes civil society and people with lived experience. Hosting should be **in-country on government infrastructure** with encrypted off-site backup. I could not confirm whether Sierra Leone has a suitable government data centre, so this needs checking.

### Offline and mobile

With roughly one in five people online and power unreliable even in Freetown, the system must assume no connection. Tablets running DHIS2 Android Capture store data encrypted and sync when a signal appears, and DHIS2 supports SMS-based reporting when there is no data connection ([DHIS2 SMS docs](https://docs.dhis2.org/en/implement/android-implementation/sms-for-reporting.html)). Outreach teams need **solar chargers or power banks**. Clients, most of whom will not have smartphones or data, interact through **USSD menus, SMS reminders and voice calls**, which work on any phone. A WhatsApp channel can serve the minority on social media, but personal data should never travel over it.

## Roll out in four phases and budget recurring costs from day one

The phasing follows the constraints: governance before software, then Freetown, where power and connectivity are concentrated, before the districts.

| Phase | Timing | What happens | Success test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Governance** | 0–6 months | NDLEA–MoHS MoU and data-sharing protocol; legal instrument; data protection impact assessment; consent scripts in Krio; governance committee; agree the build with the MoHS DHIS2 team; map indicators to WENDU and the UNODC questionnaire | Signed protocol; agreed minimum dataset |
| **1. Western Area pilot** | 6–18 months | Kissy unit, government and NGO rehab centres, 2–3 outreach teams; DHIS2 Tracker program; toll-free USSD/voice line; early-warning event program linked to drug-sample testing; aggregate NDLEA dashboard | Follow-up rate well above 37%; no privacy incidents; evidence that registration does not deter people |
| **2. High-burden districts** | 18–36 months | Expand to districts such as Bo (which already has a centre), Kenema, Bombali and Port Loko, chosen using treatment and WENDU data; solar kits; SMS fallback; train district M&E staff | Data quality maintained outside Freetown |
| **3. National integration** | 36+ months | Optional NIN linkage via the identity store; link to inpatient records and toxicology labs; automatic WENDU/UNODC reporting; full move to the government budget | Running on government funds |

On **costs**, I found **no reliable benchmarks** for a DHIS2 Tracker rollout or for USSD short-code fees in Sierra Leone, so any figure in a proposal should come from quotes from the MoHS DHIS2 team and from Orange and Africell. The cost categories are predictable: configuration and design, tablets and solar kits, training, SMS/USSD/voice fees, airtime for follow-up calls, hosting and backup, a small support team, and the data protection officer. Building on DHIS2 avoids licence fees, which matters when the NDLEA's whole budget was reported at about US$50,000. The bigger risk is **sustainability, not the upfront cost**. Research on Tanzania and Mozambique found that most donor-funded health IT projects in low-income countries fail or become unsustainable, and survive only when absorbed into ministry routines ([PMC5223327](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5223327)). Sierra Leone's own 2018–2023 digital health strategy was poorly executed ([ICTworks](https://www.ictworks.org/updated-every-african-countrys-national-digital-health-strategy-in-2026/)), and a donor-funded CommCare health worker project closed in 2022 ([World Vision](https://www.wvi.org/publications/health/2021-digital-health-factsheet-aim-health-plus-sierra-leone)). The procurement terms should therefore require government ownership of code, data and admin passwords, open standards, local support contracts, recurrent costs written into MoHS and NDLEA budget lines from year one, and a donor-exit plan before launch. Likely funders are **UNODC and the EU** (WENDU and treatment standards) ([ThisDay](https://thisdaylive.com/index.php/2019/11/04/unodc-ecowas-eu-release-wendu-report)), **WHO** (mental health), and the **World Bank** (digital ID and energy). I did not verify current funding lines from the US government or the Global Fund, and 2025 US aid cuts may have reduced them.

The main risks, and how to reduce each, are listed below.

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Data reaches police or courts | 30-year minimums make exposure catastrophic and will drive people away from care | Two compartments; aggregate-only NDLEA access; audited court-order route; legal instrument |
| System becomes a watch list | The Philippines and Thailand show the harm | No community lists; consent; retention limits; civil society oversight |
| Donor money ends | The common failure pattern | MoHS ownership; DHIS2; budget lines from year one |
| Parallel NDLEA system | Duplicates work and fragments data | One DHIS2 instance, with the NDLEA as a data consumer |
| Drug mix changes faster than records | Nitazene variants evade test strips | "Suspected vs confirmed" coding; access to a reference lab; early-warning alerts |
| Treatment capacity lags the data | Portugal's caseload drift; Ghana's unfunded law | Triage; pair the system with funded treatment and naloxone |
| Low connectivity and power | Rural electricity access is around 5–6% | Offline-first apps; SMS/USSD; solar kits |
| Headcount targets distort practice | Indonesia's quotas | Report outcomes (retention, follow-up, deaths), not just enrolments |

## What to take into the first NDLEA meeting

The strongest pitch to the Executive Director is that the NDLEA can **lead a national response without owning patient files**. Martha should open by asking what specifically impressed the NDLEA about Botswana, because the evidence suggests it was the agency-plus-rehabilitation model and the M&E framework rather than any software. She can then make three points in turn. First, Sierra Leone already has the legal hook: the never-used treatment assessment panels in Part IV of the 2008 Act. A referral pathway would put the NDLEA's own law into practice. Second, the system gives the NDLEA what it actually needs for its mission: hotspot maps, drug-composition alerts from seized and street samples, and trend data for WENDU and international reporting. Patient records stay in the health compartment, which protects the agency from blame if they leak. Third, a MoHS-hosted DHIS2 build is the only option the NDLEA's budget can sustain. A concrete first ask is a joint NDLEA–MoHS working group, which should include the NaTFDSA, to sign a data-sharing protocol and agree a minimum dataset, followed by a funded Western Area pilot. Before any of this goes into a formal proposal, the facts drawn from search snippets should be checked against the original pages: the Executive Director's appointment, the 212-bed and 37% figures (from an unreviewed preprint), the status of the data protection bill, and the practice direction.

## Conclusion

The most useful shift in thinking is that "a system for drug users" is mainly a **trust problem, with technology second**. Every working model, from England's consent form to Kenya's accreditation register, earns its data by convincing people that coming forward is safe and worthwhile. Every failed model, from Russia's registry to the Philippine watch lists, collapsed because it did the opposite. Sierra Leone's sentencing regime raises the stakes above almost any country studied, so the firewall between treatment and enforcement is the feature that decides whether the system works at all.

The second insight is that Sierra Leone has a short window to do something new. No African country, Botswana included, has a national, interoperable, privacy-protected drug-treatment record with outcome follow-up and a drug-checking alert loop. Sierra Leone already has the DHIS2 base, a coordinating task force, a growing network of centres and a law that provides for treatment orders. If the NDLEA convenes and MoHS hosts, the country could build the first such system in the region. Neighbours facing the same kush market, starting with Liberia, could then adopt it, which would also make it a stronger case for regional donor funding.
